# Where the source method conflicts with UK law

Zach trades in the UK — Europe/London, HMRC, VAT-registered. Several practices
recur across the Whop course and both YouTube channels that are **unlawful or
platform-breaking in that jurisdiction**. They are recorded here because they are
genuinely part of the method being taught, and leaving them out would make this
knowledge base quietly dishonest about its own source.

**SPZ must never recommend, plan, draft or assist with any of the items below.**
If Zach raises one, say plainly that it is in the source, that it is unlawful or
account-ending here, and offer the legitimate alternative. Do not moralise; state
it once and move on.

This is not legal advice, and the specifics change — point him at the CMA or a
solicitor for anything he actually intends to act on.

## 1. Fake reviews — illegal, and the most repeated practice in the corpus

Where it appears:
- The 4h free course: self-staged photos used as customer reviews, and importing
  reviews from AliExpress/Amazon onto his own store.
- The 8h free course: fake reviews called acceptable provided you do not lie about
  shipping times or capabilities.
- The 60-minute store build: every review in the demo is fabricated — invented
  names, countries and star ratings, with a "300+ reviews" badge on a store that
  has never sold anything.

**The law:** the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 made fake
reviews an explicitly banned practice. Writing them, commissioning them, or
publishing them without taking reasonable steps to verify they are genuine all
fall within it. The CMA can act directly, with penalties reaching a share of
global turnover.

**The legitimate version:** seed real reviews through post-purchase email
requests, which the same sources already teach for other reasons. It is slower and
it is the only lawful route.

## 2. "Compare at" pricing anchored to a price never charged

The store build sets a $24.99 price against a $34.99 "compare at", where the
referenced prior price was $27.99. A reference price must be one genuinely charged
for a meaningful period; inventing or inflating it is misleading-pricing conduct
under the same Act.

**The legitimate version:** only show a "was" price you actually charged, for long
enough to be real.

## 3. Rage-bait ghost accounts / phantom accounts

Two videos describe running separate accounts to post inflammatory comments and
replies under your own posts to farm engagement, timed after a video gains
traction so the platform does not flag same-device manipulation.

Not a criminal matter, but a straightforward breach of platform terms on both
Instagram and TikTok, and the stated reason for the timing is explicitly to evade
detection. For a business whose entire distribution is organic reach, an
enforcement action is existential — this risks the whole operation to boost one
video.

## 4. Reposting mirrored/brightness-shifted videos as new

Milder, and widely done. Still algorithm manipulation and still against platform
guidance on re-uploaded content. Worth knowing the risk sits with the account.

## What this does NOT taint

The great majority of the method is ordinary marketing craft and is unaffected:
the creative structure, hooks, the variable-isolation testing discipline, product
selection, the diagnostic funnel, pricing psychology that does not misrepresent a
prior price, store layout, account warm-up, and the influencer outreach process.

Flagging these four is not a judgement on the source. It is that Zach is building a
real UK business with a VAT registration and a bank connection, and the downside of
these specific items lands on him, not on the person teaching them.
